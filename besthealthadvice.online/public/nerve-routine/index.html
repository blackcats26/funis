Nerve health

Tingling, "pins and needles" feet at night? What researchers know about the nutrients your nerves run on

Nerves are some of the most energy-hungry cells in the body. Here's what peer-reviewed studies say about alpha lipoic acid, carnitine and other nutrients, and the simple evening routine some adults are adding.

Sponsored content by Best Health AdvicePublished November 16, 20266 min read

It often starts quietly. A little tingling in the toes when you lie down. Feet that feel cold, numb or "asleep" for no reason. A burning or prickly sensation that seems worse at night, right when you want to rest.

Researchers have spent decades studying the long, delicate nerves that reach our hands and feet, and one theme keeps coming up: these nerves depend heavily on steady energy, good circulation and protection from oxidative stress.

Sound familiar?

  • Tingling or "pins and needles" in the toes or feet
  • Discomfort that seems worse in the evening or at night
  • Feet that often feel cold or numb
  • A burning or prickly feeling after a long day on your feet

These sensations can have many causes, some of them serious. If you notice them, especially new or worsening symptoms, see your doctor for an evaluation. Nothing in this article replaces medical care.

Why the nerves in your feet are so demanding

Some of the nerves that reach your toes are among the longest cells in the body. Keeping signals moving along that distance takes a constant supply of energy, oxygen-rich blood flow, and protection from the oxidative stress that naturally builds up with age.

Three things healthy nerves rely on

  1. EnergyNerve cells depend on mitochondria, the "power plants" inside each cell.
  2. CirculationTiny blood vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients to the nerves.
  3. ProtectionAntioxidants help defend cells from everyday oxidative stress.

This is why researchers have looked closely at nutrients involved in cellular energy, circulation and antioxidant defense, and why several of them are now studied for nerve comfort.

What the research says

Here are peer-reviewed studies on the same types of ingredients discussed below.

Alpha lipoic acid and nerve-related symptoms

In a randomized, placebo-controlled trial (SYDNEY 2), adults with diabetes-related nerve discomfort who took 600 mg of oral alpha lipoic acid daily for five weeks reported greater improvement in symptoms such as burning, pain and numbness than those taking placebo.1

Diabetes Care, 2006

Carnitine and nerve fibers

In two 52-week randomized, placebo-controlled trials, acetyl-L-carnitine (a form of carnitine) was associated with less reported pain, better vibration perception and signs of nerve fiber regeneration in adults with long-term diabetes-related nerve problems.2

Diabetes Care, 2005

Curcumin and oxidative stress

A scientific review of human research describes curcumin, the active compound in turmeric, as supporting the body's response to oxidative stress and inflammation.3

Foods, 2017

Butcher's broom and circulation in the legs

A meta-analysis of 20 clinical trials found that a preparation containing butcher's broom (Ruscus aculeatus), hesperidin methyl chalcone and vitamin C reduced leg heaviness, cramps and swelling in people with chronic venous insufficiency.4

International Angiology, 2003

Magnesium and CoQ10: nerve signaling and cellular energy

Scientific reviews describe magnesium's role in nerve transmission and muscle function,5 and coenzyme Q10's role in mitochondrial energy production, which naturally declines with age.6

Physiological Reviews, 2015 · Frontiers in Physiology, 2018
